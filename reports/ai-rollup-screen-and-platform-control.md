## The answer

**The thesis survives the screen. The screen moves the opportunity, and it moves it away
from the pipeline CSI has.** Four findings, in order of how much they change what CSI does.

**1. Under the AI-roll-up screen, overlap beats complement — which inverts the November
bias explicitly.** `reports/acquisition-target-longlist.md` §1 states "a deliberate bias
toward complement over overlap": buying a corridor CSI does not serve buys something new,
buying more MIA buys CSI's own strength twice. That is correct for a conventional bolt-on
and wrong for this thesis. Platform leverage comes from putting *more of the same
rule-bound work* through a system that already encodes its rules. A new corridor needs new
consular requirements, a new country rulebook, new carrier relationships — a new platform
module, and under FreightFlow a new Change Order. A same-gateway, same-corridor,
same-paperwork target rides the platform at near-zero marginal build cost, which is the
mechanism behind the one hard data point in the four sources (Long Lake's add-ons going
from a year to days, grounding §1).

**2. Nothing on the longlist carries a platform.** Every entry is a book, a corridor or an
identity to be established. Not one brings an owned software layer or an engineering team.
So on the pipeline as it stands, acquisitions buy EBITDA at a multiple — which
`reports/the-25m-gap.md` says is required and worth doing — but they do not re-rate CSI.
**The re-rating has to come from the platform CSI does not control.** That is the task's own
line to the metric, and the answer is no.

**3. The highest-scoring entry in Part B is a category with no company in it.** Gateway
trade services and ship-out preparation centres score highest on the roll-up criteria for a
*different* reason than November gave them: almost entirely rule-bound document and
coordination work, same workflow as CSI's desk, no entrenched systems to unwind, small
enough to absorb. November found no nameable target in public sources and concluded they
will not be found on the web. That conclusion is reinforced. **The action that unlocks the
top of the pipeline is a free internal questionnaire to Kim Soto and Susej Roffe** — item 2
of November's §7, still not done.

**4. CSI's fit is weaker than five-of-six, and the reason is a link the grounding report
scores as two independent rows.** See §A.4 and §D.1. The category-growth failure does not
just fail one criterion; it partly undermines the one scored Strong. Whether that is fatal
is market-intel's call (handoff raised), not this report's — but the screen says what
follows either way.

**On Part C: the platform-control cost is unknown, and this report declines to invent it.**
Six routes are set out as a decision structure. For each: what it is, what has to be true,
what is knowable about cost today, and the single fact that would most change it. Four of
those six facts are held inside CSI or by counsel and **none requires contact with the
Developer.** Until they are gathered, no route is priceable, and gathering them is the whole
of the near-term work.

**One refinement to the brief, stated plainly as `§8` requires.** The brief and the grounding
report both treat platform control as *gating* for an acquisition programme. On the text, the
Change Order requirement is a **mutual** agreement — neither side may classify unilaterally
(`freightflow-agreement-gaps.md` row 7). So the Developer is not obliged to agree, and CSI is
not obliged to pay whatever is asked. The practical exposure is **cost and latency per deal,
not a veto.** That makes platform control *pricing* for a first deal and *gating* for a
programme, because unbounded per-deal negotiation cannot be underwritten. The distinction
matters because it changes the order of operations: a pre-agreed scope and rate for migration
work — one negotiation, not one per deal — is a cheaper answer than ownership and belongs at
the top of Route 1.

# Part A — the screen

## A.1 What this screen is for, and what it is not for

**It is a pre-contact research screen. Its output is a research ranking, not a buy ranking.**
That is not hedging; it is forced by the criteria themselves. Long Lake's primary screen —
*"very high logo retention and net revenue retention above 100%"* (Fig, via grounding §1) —
is unobservable for a private company from public sources. So is EBITDA contribution, which
`reports/the-25m-gap.md` §6 correctly names as the criterion that matters most.

So the work splits into two stages and conflating them is the error this section exists to
prevent:

| Stage | Decides | Criteria | Source of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-contact screen** (this report) | Who is worth further research, and in what order | A.3 below, public sources only | Public records, trade directories, registries, trade press |
| **Post-contact gate** (Ethan's conversation, later) | Who is worth an approach, and on what structure | EBITDA contribution, margin quality, true retention and NRR, cost to serve, systems reality | Seller-supplied, diligenced |

Weighting EBITDA heavily in the pre-contact screen would score every target identically
unknown and feel rigorous. It is in the screen at a low weight, scored only on an observable
*proxy* for margin mix, and labelled as such.

**Score** = weighted sum over scored criteria ÷ maximum possible over scored criteria × 100.
Unknowable criteria are excluded from both, so a thin target is not punished with a low score
— it is punished with low **coverage**, which is reported next to the score and is the number
to read first.

## A.4 Evidence tags — the honesty mechanic

Every criterion for every target carries one tag. This is how Part B separates what was
observed from what was inferred, as the task requires.

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **O** | A **public fact about this target**, with a source cited in the November longlist |
| **I** | An **inference from a public fact about this target** |
| **A** | A **category assumption** — true of businesses of this type, with no target-specific fact behind it |
| **U** | **Unknown.** Scored 0 and excluded from the score; it lowers coverage instead |

Reported per target: **score**, **coverage** (share of total weight scored at all), and the
split **O / I / A**. A high score resting mostly on **A** is a hypothesis about a category
wearing a target's name.

## A.5 Four things this screen does that the November screen did not

1. **Overlap is rewarded, complement is penalised.** S1's "same work" sub-test and S4's
   entanglement penalty between them reverse the November bias. The mechanism is Long Lake's
   falling marginal cost per add-on, and in CSI's case it is sharpened by the fact that a new
   rulebook means a Change Order a third party can price or decline.
2. **S3 and S4 deliberately conflict.** A paper-and-phone operation scores badly on
   extractability and well on entanglement: its book transfers cleanly and its history does
   not. A target with its own platform scores the reverse. **Neither is disqualifying and the
   trade-off is a real one** — if CSI needs volume on the platform, low entanglement wins; if
   it needs a data layer to automate against, extractability wins. Which matters more depends
   on a number CSI has not measured (§D.1).
3. **Scale is not a criterion.** `reports/the-25m-gap.md` §6: EBITDA contribution and margin
   quality beat revenue scale. Larger targets score *worse* here, through S4 and S5.
4. **"Market growing faster than GDP" is not a per-target criterion.** Dewez's screen applies
   to the category, and CSI's category fails it (grounding §1). It is a gate on the thesis,
   handled in §D.1, not a column that would score the same for everyone.

## A.6 What the screen cannot see, stated once

- **True logo retention and NRR, for any target.** Long Lake's primary screen. Post-contact.
- **Automatable share as a measured figure, for any target.** Only the service-mix proxy.
- **EBITDA, margin, cost to serve, for any target.** Post-contact.
- **Whether the owner would sell, at all.** S5 measures the *conditions* of availability, not
  the fact of it. Patwa's point stands: nobody had thought to ask the wife.
- **Integration capacity.** No target is called integrable in this report and none should be
  until `operations` answers November's handoff. The FreightFlow Change Order cost applies to
  **every** target equally, so it discriminates between doing a deal and not doing one — not
  between targets.

### C.1a One refinement on D2, and it lowers the problem

The brief and the grounding report both treat the Developer's ability to decline an integration

migration work.** One negotiation instead of one per acquisition. It converts an unbounded,
unpriceable risk into a known cost, which is what "underwritable" means. It appears in neither
legal-risk report's remedy column and it is the first thing on Route 1.

## D.4 Where this report disagrees with the grounding report

`§8` requires this directly rather than politely. Five items. **None of them changes the
grounding report's conclusion; three change a decision.**

**1. "Markets where better service wins share rather than price" is scored *Mixed*, and should
be *Strong with a caveat*.** The grounding report's reason is that CSI's lead differentiator —
no booking fee — is *"a price claim."* On CSI's own facts it is not a price claim about the
good being sold: funeral homes pay **the same air rate they would pay the airline direct**
(`CLAUDE.md` §1), and the competitive axis is **whether a brokerage fee exists at all** —
Eagle's Wings is observed at roughly 10% of published rate (`§4.2`). That is a packaging and
inclusion claim, not price competition on the underlying rate. Since this is the criterion the
grounding report treats as CSI's second-weakest, scoring it Mixed understates the fit.
**Decision impact: low. Correctness impact: real.**

**2. The 30% subscription share is under-weighted, and it is the term that matters most for the
platform story.** Grounding §2 row 4 makes the revenue share the headline — *"the more
successfully CSI becomes a platform company, the more it pays a third party for the privilege,
at a rising rate."* Directionally right, but the magnitude invites a wrong inference. At $2M of
portal-originated revenue the obligation is about **$45,000 a year** against roughly $15.0M of
revenue: **material to a multiple and to the narrative, trivial to cash flow.** The term that
genuinely threatens the platform story is **30% of subscription fees** (Ex. C §6), which is an
order of magnitude more aggressive and sits directly on the SaaS path that would justify a
re-rating. It appears in grounding §2 only by reference. **Decision impact: it changes what
Route 4 should prioritise.**

**3. "Gating" overstates the Change Order problem for a first deal.** Grounding §2 row 3 and
the task brief both treat the Developer's ability to decline an integration as gating. The text
supports the narrower reading: §2.4 and §19.5 require **mutual** written agreement, so neither
side may classify unilaterally — the Developer need not agree **and CSI need not pay whatever is
asked** (`freightflow-agreement-gaps.md` row 7 says exactly this). The real exposure is **cost
and latency per deal**, which makes it *pricing* for one deal and *gating* for a programme,
because unbounded per-deal negotiation cannot be underwritten. **Decision impact: high — it
puts a pre-agreed migration scope and rate at the top of Route 1 as a cheaper answer than
ownership.**

**4. The category-growth failure and the capacity-constraint strength are not independent, and
scoring them as two rows overstates CSI's fit.** Grounding §1 scores *"growing faster than
GDP"* as **Fails** (cremation erodes long-distance transport) and *"capacity-constrained
workforce"* as **Strong** (nine-person desk, burnout named, *"the capacity release has somewhere
obvious to go"*). **The second depends on the first.** Fig's mechanism is that capacity taken as
growth compounds — but growth requires demand to absorb it. In a structurally shrinking category
released capacity has nowhere to go except share taken from competitors, which is slower and
contested, or into adjacent lines (the assistance channel, documentation-as-a-service, the SaaS
path) which grounding §1 itself calls *"plausible, unquantified."* So the "somewhere obvious to
go" is **not obvious** — it is unsized. **CSI's fit is better read as four-of-six with one
Strong conditional, not five-of-six.** This is the most substantive disagreement in this list,
and it raises the value of market-intel's answer and of sizing the assistance channel.
**Decision impact: high.**

**5. Grounding §4's table omits the one measurement that prices two of the Part C routes.** The
portal-revenue reconciliation — Donald's open item from `freightflow-agreement-review.md` §7 —
is the largest single input to Routes 2 and 4, is cheap, and is not in the "what nobody can
answer yet" table. **It belongs there.** `/brain-propose` is not the right instrument (this is a
report, not a brain fact); it is raised as a handoff to `finance` instead.
