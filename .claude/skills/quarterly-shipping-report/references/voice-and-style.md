# Voice & Style

## Who you're writing for

Funeral directors, owners, and the corporate executives above them. They are operators who serve grieving families every day. They respect competence, plain speech, and discretion. They are skeptical of vendors and allergic to hype. Many have moved remains for decades and will spot a writer who doesn't understand the work.

Write so that:
- A director skims the bold headlines in 30 seconds and gets value.
- A corporate VP forwards it internally without a second thought.
- A reader who already uses a competitor still finds it worth reading.

## The voice in three words

**Authoritative. Dignified. Useful.**

- **Authoritative** — confident framing, no hedging filler ("we think maybe," "it seems like"). State what the data shows. Confidence lives in the framing, never in invented precision.
- **Dignified** — this is the non-negotiable one. See below.
- **Useful** — every section earns its place by teaching or helping. If a paragraph doesn't inform or help the reader, cut it.

## Dignity is the brand (non-negotiable)

The subject is deceased people and the families who love them. The readers do sacred work. Tone failures here don't just read badly — they destroy CSI's credibility with this exact audience and contradict who CSI is.

- Say **"loved ones," "families," "the deceased," "final journey," "repatriation," "brought home."**
- Never **"units," "product," "cargo" (when referring to a person), "bodies" used coldly,** or anything that turns volume into a boast detached from the people it represents.
- Numbers are fine and necessary — frame them as *families served* and *journeys completed*, not inventory moved. "CSI helped bring more than [N] loved ones home this quarter" — not "we processed N units."
- Re-read the whole draft once with only this question: *would a grieving family or a veteran director find any sentence cold?* Fix anything that fails.

## Insight first, CSI second

Lead with what's true about the industry. CSI is the byline and the data source, not the hero. The credibility comes from being right and helpful; the soft message ("a partner who sees these patterns can plan around them") lands on its own. Roughly: 90% industry insight, 10% understated CSI presence.

## Mechanics

- Short paragraphs. Generous white space. Bold the headlines and the few numbers that matter.
- Active voice. Concrete nouns. No corporate filler ("leverage synergies," "best-in-class solutions").
- Define nothing the audience already knows (they know what a consular legalization is); explain anything genuinely technical in one clause.
- One idea per paragraph. Let the structure carry the authority.

## Before / after

- Cold → Warm
  - ✗ "CSI processed 3,100 units in Q1, a 4% volume increase."
  - ✓ "CSI helped bring home more than 3,100 loved ones this quarter — modestly more than last, with the strongest demand on the Latin America corridors."
- Hype → Authority
  - ✗ "CSI is the #1 best human remains shipping solution in the industry!"
  - ✓ "Across eight U.S. gateways, the pattern this quarter was clear: routing flexibility, not price alone, decided how quickly a family could bring a loved one home."
- Hedge → Confident
  - ✗ "We kind of think maybe the holidays might affect some corridors a little."
  - ✓ "Expect the Mexico and Central America corridors to tighten ahead of the holidays — book early."
- Salesy → Useful
  - ✗ "Call CSI today for the best rates — don't get ripped off by interline!"
  - ✓ "Interline bookings still routinely take three days or more to confirm at top-tariff pricing. If a family is on a tight timeline, ask any partner how fast they can confirm — and at what rate."

## A note on competitors

Never name and disparage Eagle's Wings Air, Inman, ILS, or others. The report wins by being the most useful and most authoritative voice in the room. Let the quality do the positioning.
